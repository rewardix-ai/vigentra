05The signal chain

On screen
Six numbered stages, each showing the real data at that step, two YES/NO decisions, and outcomes with real refused plates: Not read, No plate saved. A square follows one car; the event log says what each stage does as it arrives. Red squares take the NO lines.
Say
Here is the whole method on one screen, following that real car. BEAT The camera gives us video. We find the car and track it, so it keeps one identity. Inside the car, and only inside it, we look for the plate. We keep its best frames and combine them. BEAT Then the first decision: are there enough pixels? If not, it is not read at all. If yes, we read the plate. BEAT Then the second decision: do the readings agree, and is it a valid Indian format? If not, no plate is saved. PAUSE Only when both answers are yes do we have a confirmed plate: the registration, the time and the camera. Never the video. The red squares you see are plates that took a NO line: that is the system declining to guess.

Under the hood

20What Vigentra is built with

On screen
Eight panels: capture (RTSP over TCP), vehicles (YOLO11s + ByteTrack), plates (YOLO11n inside each vehicle), reading (two CRNN readers + PaddleOCR), deciding (votes, plate rules, glyph check), central (FastAPI, PostgreSQL), console (Next.js, Leaflet, OpenStreetMap), delivery (Docker).
Say
Under the hood, every part is open source: no licence fee, no vendor account. BEAT Video comes in over RTSP, the protocol the cameras already speak. YOLO11 finds the vehicles, the larger model for the recorded results and the lighter one on the live reader, and ByteTrack gives each vehicle an identity, so its plate is decided once. The plate detector looks only inside the vehicle. Two kinds of reader read it, CRNN models and PaddleOCR, because different readers make different mistakes. BEAT Then the vote, the Indian plate rules and a check that real character shapes are present decide. The centre is FastAPI and PostgreSQL, holding no video and no camera passwords. The console is Next.js on OpenStreetMap, and it all ships in Docker.
Land this
Every part is a deliberate, open choice.

21Why this, not that

On screen
A table of seven decisions: what Vigentra uses, instead of what, and why it is better.
Say
Every choice was made against an alternative, and here is why ours wins. The AI runs next to the video, not in one central data centre: that is forty to two hundred and fifty times less network. A plate is read once per vehicle, from all its frames: the single-frame reader we started with found one plate in sixty-seven vehicles. Only what many frames agree on is saved, which is how we keep wrong plates at zero. Two kinds of reader together read sixteen plates where one read thirteen. ByteTrack, because fixed cameras need no camera-motion correction. Watchlist matching weighted by real OCR errors, so one wrong character still raises the alert. And video is brokered, so revoking someone's access stops their stream within one segment.
Land this
Each choice is measured or reasoned, not fashion.

22What we tested and dropped

On screen
Six rows: confirming on 3 crops (a wrong plate on Delhi), 20 crops (no gain), two CRNNs backing each other (shared mistakes), frames enlarged to 1920 px (half the vehicles, slower), a plate box up to 0.9 of its vehicle (a hoarding read), one frame at a time (1 plate from 67 vehicles).
Say
Just as important is what we tried and threw away, because each was measured on the same clips before we decided. Confirming a plate on three crops instead of four let a wrong plate through on Delhi, so it stays at four. Twenty crops instead of twelve found nothing more. Our two CRNN readers share mistakes, so they are not allowed to vouch for each other. Enlarging every frame to nineteen-twenty pixels halved the vehicles found and slowed everything down. A loose plate box let a hoarding be read as a plate. And reading one frame at a time found one plate in sixty-seven vehicles. Nothing went in on a hunch.

25What it needs

On screen
Two big numbers: about 160 Gbps if video went central, about 1 Gbps with Vigentra; then the requirements table, tier by tier: edge GPUs, regional, central, storage, integrations, people, cost.
Say
And here is what it takes, sized honestly. If every camera's video were sent to one data centre, you would need about a hundred and sixty gigabits a second of network, and storage for about one point seven petabytes of video every day. Vigentra sends only metadata: about one gigabit a second for the whole state. BEAT What it does need: about three thousand GPU nodes at the edge, in the departments' own sites, with plate reading only on cameras whose view allows it. Two nodes per district. Two central sites, one a disaster-recovery copy. Metadata kept hot for thirty days, warm for a year, cold for seven, with the audit log kept in full. Access to each department's video system and the state network. People in every district to confirm alerts, and a small team labelling footage. The edge GPUs are the one large cost, ninety to a hundred and twenty crore over the phases; the software has no licence fees.
